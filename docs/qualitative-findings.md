# Qualitative comparison: available Phil Ivey hands

## Scope and method

This review covers the first nine hero-visible snapshots in [`reports/qualitative-ivey.json`](../reports/qualitative-ivey.json), reviewed under `fast-heuristic-v2`. The packet also includes a separately labeled video snapshot covered in [`video-comparison.md`](video-comparison.md). The recorded code fingerprint identifies the policy that generated a packet; later strategy revisions require a fresh review. For each choice here, I use only Ivey's cards, the board, public actions already taken, position, pot, price and stacks. Opponent hole cards, later cards, later actions and the result are not evidence for an earlier choice. The two structured-source hands illustrate choices; they cannot establish Ivey's overall frequencies, intent, or which line was best.

The first four decisions come from the machine-readable [2009 Dwan–Ivey PHH record](https://github.com/uoftcprg/phh-dataset/blob/main/data/dwan-ivey-2009.phh). The next five are a separate reconstruction from the [CardPlayer Ivey–Polk report](https://www.cardplayer.com/poker-tools/hand-matchups/1921). That reconstruction assumes the reported 3,000 ante was paid by each of four players, infers folds by UTG and the small blind from the reported heads-up continuation, and assumes no unreported calls. Its decisions are therefore less certain than the PHH sequence; the source report does not supply a complete machine-readable hand history. This review uses only those public sources; no private Ivey archive was available, and footage of these two hands was not verified for this review.

The report packet's neutral protocol uses `raise` for any aggressive action. Where Ivey acts with `to_call = 0`, that means a bet or lead in ordinary poker terms; where he faces a wager, it means a raise. A displayed amount is the **total target for the street**. “Added chips” are that target minus Ivey's existing street contribution; the raise portion beyond a call is smaller when Ivey already owes chips.

## Four PHH decisions: Dwan–Ivey

1. **Preflop, A♣2♦ in the small blind:** Facing the button's raise, with the big blind still to act, Ivey reraises to 23,000. `fast` folds: its random-hand preflop estimate falls from 57.1% to 49.1% after its fixed range adjustment, just under its 50% continue cutoff, so the recorded action has zero probability in its mix. One plausible explanation for Ivey's reraise is ace-blocker pressure against a potentially wide button opening range; another is that the out-of-position, multiway risk makes folding attractive. The packet cannot tell us the button's range or Ivey's read. The target is 23,000 total, 22,000 new chips over the posted small blind; 6,000 of those match the call, leaving a 16,000 reraise beyond it. `fast`'s pure fold shows a narrow continue policy here, not proof that either choice is wrong.

2. **Flop, J♣3♦5♣:** With no bet to call and 49,500 in the pot, Ivey bets 35,000 with ace-high and a four-out wheel draw. `fast` checks; it sees no pair or flush draw and assigns only 28.9% heuristic equity, despite the straight outs. The bet can represent the preflop aggressor's stronger range and pressure unpaired hands; it can also build a large pot while risking a call from pairs or draws. Its 35,000 is new chips, about 70.7% of the pot. The heuristic's pure check is consistent with its hand-category focus, but it does not weigh the raiser's range advantage or fold equity.

3. **Turn, 4♥:** Ivey now has A-2-3-4-5, a five-high wheel, and bets 90,000 into 119,500. `fast` also selects aggression, assigning the action class 50.75% probability, but targets 65,725 (55% pot). This is the one PHH choice where the recorded action class is in the bot's mix; the size is still materially smaller. A larger bet may charge draws or get value from strong one-pair/two-pair hands, while a smaller bet may keep weaker hands in and risk less. The wheel is strong but sits at the low end of the straight range: a hand such as 6-7 could already have a higher straight on this visible turn. That risk is apparent from the board and prior line, without using any later reveal. The packet cannot resolve how much weight Ivey placed on value, protection, fold equity, or a read.

4. **Turn, facing the button's raise:** After Ivey's 90,000 bet, the button raises to 232,600. Ivey owes 142,600 into a 442,100 pot; `fast` calls and gives no probability to a reraise. It reports 79% raw heuristic equity, discounts this to 72% for pressure, and calls above its 26.4% continue threshold. Its reraise gate starts at 73%, so the heuristic just misses it. Ivey instead shoves to a 1,067,100 street total. That adds 977,100 over his existing 90,000; 142,600 of the addition would call, leaving 834,500 as the raise beyond a call. The extra raise is 142.7% of the 584,700 pot after calling. The shove applies maximum pressure but risks being called by the higher end of the opponent's range; the call preserves the chance to realize the wheel's showdown value. Neither response can be judged without an opponent range and response model. The brittle 72%/73% transition is a policy behavior worth testing, not evidence of a true strategic boundary.

## Five reconstructed decisions: Ivey–Polk

The CardPlayer report describes a four-handed final table at 12,000/24,000 blinds with a 3,000 ante. It reports a button raise to 55,000, Ivey's big-blind call, and the later street sequence below. In the reconstructed preflop snapshot, Ivey holds T♣9♦, the pot is 103,000 and he owes 31,000. `fast` calls too: its range-adjusted preflop estimate is 43.2%, above its 42% cutoff. That is action agreement, but its simple price-and-range calculation does not model the final-table payout utility; those payouts are not available in the snapshot.

On the **T♥3♠2♦ flop**, Ivey checks top pair after defending preflop, and `fast` checks as well. Checking can protect the big blind's range and leave the button room to continue; leading could also extract value or deny a free card. The subsequent button check is not part of Ivey's information at this decision. The matching check comes from a generic check branch, not from a model of the button's range.

On the **2♥ turn**, after the flop checked through, Ivey leads 75,000 into 134,000 (56.0% pot). `fast` checks: its 59% hand-and-board heuristic is below the 62% threshold for a value-bet mix, and its small-bluff condition excludes this paired board and big-blind position. With T-9, the bet could seek thin value or protection from overcards and weaker made hands; checking avoids putting more chips in when a turn check-back may conceal strength. The snapshot supports both possibilities, but `fast` has no explicit range update for the check-back.

On the **6♣ river**, after Ivey's turn bet was called, he leads 200,000 into 284,000 (70.4% pot). `fast` again checks, with 56.5% heuristic equity below its betting threshold. The prior call makes the opponent's range more consequential, while the 6 can complete a straight for 4-5. Ivey's bet could be thin value or pressure; it can also be called by stronger hands. A generic hand-category score and fixed cutoff do not represent those competing ranges. The two consecutive lead disagreements are the clearest example here of `fast`'s narrow value/protection aggression.

Finally, after the river lead is raised to 580,000, Ivey faces 380,000 into a 1,064,000 pot, or 26.3% immediate pot odds. Both Ivey and `fast` call. The bot's 49.5% adjusted heuristic clears its 28.3% continue threshold, but that is not a range-based equity or an EV estimate. A call depends on how often the raise is a bluff or overplayed hand versus value, and on tournament utility. The public sequence alone does not establish the right mix.

## Policy hypotheses and next experiments

These are general behaviors in `fast.py` to investigate on **unseen hands**, not branches to add for these labels:

1. **Postflop decisions are weakly conditioned on opponent ranges and action history.** Equity is estimated mostly from hero's made-hand category, draw outs and board texture; opponent aggression is counted only on the current street. The checked-through flop, turn call and raise sequence illustrate history that should change ranges. First, assemble held-out hero-visible states across complete hands and compare otherwise similar nodes after different public lines (check-back, bet-call, raise). Verify that the policy's action mix changes for a reason tied to that evidence, without revealing opponent cards.
2. **Thin-value and protection bets have narrow gates.** The no-bet branch uses fixed 0.62/0.72 equity cutoffs and excludes some small-bluff cases based on position and board flags. Sweep one-pair and two-pair states across position, checked-through lines and board textures; record when the policy checks despite plausible worse calls or vulnerable equity. Keep this separate from evaluating whether a particular recorded bet was correct.
3. **Pressure handling may be discontinuous.** A fixed pressure discount and 0.73 reraise gate turn a strong-hand call into a pure call just below the threshold in the wheel state. Perturb only price, prior aggression and heuristic strength around the cutoffs, then inspect whether action probabilities change smoothly and sensibly across held-out states.
4. **Bet sizing uses a small strength-to-pot template.** The PHH turn bet is 75.3% pot while the bot's selected size is 55%; the code largely selects 38% or 55% when unopposed. Compare size distributions on held-out public histories stratified by board connectivity, position, pot size and stack depth. Evaluate action class and size separately.
5. **Tournament utility is absent from this policy interface.** The reconstructed hand is a final-table spot, but the bot has no payout or ICM inputs. Once a payout structure and all relevant stacks are known, compare the policy's pot-odds choices with decisions that account for tournament utility; do not treat the reconstructed call as evidence of either model's superiority.

For future hand reviews, freeze the hero-only snapshot before consulting the recorded move, describe the visible price and ranges, then compare (a) action-class probability and (b) size. This makes qualitative comparison repeatable without turning a handful of selected hands into a similarity score or a verdict about intent.
